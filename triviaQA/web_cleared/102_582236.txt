I don't have an answer tot he questions posed at the OP, but I'm sorry to say you're wrong on several counts, r2.
(1) A woman can indeed be a countess in her own right (which is what you call a female earl), or even a duchess in her own right, either by being created so by the sovereign or if she inherits a title that carries through the female line. The last duchess in her own right was the Duchess of Fife, who died in 1959 and who was the oldest daughter of the Duke of Fife, who had left no sons to inherit his title.
(2) Disraeli was indeed given an earldom rather than a barony (which is lower) or a baronetcy (even lower): Queen Victoria made him Earl of Beaconsfield.
(3) Margaret Thatcher has a barony, which is one step above a baronetcy. The former is a peerage; the later is not. She is formally Baroness Thatcher of Keveston in the County of Lincolnshire.

The eldest son of an Earl *can* have a title if it is a hereditary peerage and the earldom comes with a secondary title that is given as a courtesy title to the heir. Thus the eldest son of the Earl of Wessex is the Viscount Severn; the eldest son of the Countess of Dysart (an earldom that can pass in the female line) is Lord Hightower.
The ranks of the hereditary peerage in the UK are in this order, from highest to lowest:
Dukes / Duchesses
Marquises (also called Marquesses) / Marchionesses
Earls / Countesses
Viscounts / Viscountesses
Barons / Baronesses (addressed as Lord / Lady)
Below the peerage (members of the aristocracy able to be elected to the House of Lords) are:
Baronets / Baronetesses (addressed as Sir / lady)
Below this are the knights, which is the lowest honorific title and which is not hereditary, though it does entitle your wife to the title of "Lady X" if you are "Sir X." If you are a female given a knighthood in your own right (like Julie Andrews) you are "Dame X" and your husband is not granted a title.
The direct heir of a Duke is usually granted the courtesy title of Marquess or Earl (or Marchioness or Countess if it is a title that can pass in the female line: the Dukedom of Marlborough, for example, can pass through the female line, so if there are no male direct heirs the eldest daughter is granted the courtesy title of Marchioness of Huntington).
The eldest direct heir of an earl is granted the courtesy title of Viscount / Viscountess or of Baron / Baroness (tus the eldest son of the Earl Spencer, the title given to Princess Diana's father and brother, is always called the Viscount Althorp).
The eldest direct heir of a viscount is sometimes granted the courtesy of Baron / Baroness but also sometimes has no other title.
The other children of Dukes / Duchesses, Marquesses or Marquises / Marchionesses, and earls / Countesses are addressed by the courtesy title of "Lord / Lady ____" (thus Princess Diana was Lady Diana Spencer because she was a daughter of the earl Spencer; in Brideshead Revisited, Sebastian Flyte is called Lord Sebastian because he is the younger son of the Marquis of Marchmain)
The other children of viscounts / viscountesses and of barons / baronesses are addressed by the honorific "the Honourable ______"
All the children of baronets and knights are usually not addressed by honorific titles.
Got all that? Good!

So how can a prince also be a duke?"
Because you can hold more than one title simultaneously.
Thus Prince Philip is also Duke of Edinburgh; Prince Andrew is also Duke of York; etc.
"Can one person be an earl and a baron?"
Yes. Prince Charles, for example, is both Earl of Carrick and Baron of Renfrew as well as Prince of Wales, Duke of Cornwall, Duke of Rothesay, Lord of the Islies, and a bunch of other things besides.
"Why is Prince Phillip a Prince and not King, given that he's married to the Queen?"
In some countries that has happened, and the husband of the female sovereign is given the title of King but is not himself the sovereign: for example, Lord Darnley, the husband of Mary, Queen of Scots, was referred to during his marriage to the queen (who probably had him killed, btw), as "the king," though it was understood he only held the crown matrimonial.
In England--and in the United Kingdom--the people have not wanted this to be the case for the most part. An exception was made for William II, the husband of Mary II, because he was also in the royal family of Great Britain and because they wanted to rule jointly to consolidate their power when they deposed her father, James II. But Mary's sister Queen Anne had a husband who only went by the title of Prince George of Denmark, which was the title he brought to his marriage to her.
Queen Victoria wanted Albert to be made King, but the British parliament would have none of it, so he was instead awarded a special title of Prince Consort that no one else has yet had. Seeing this problem arising again, George VI had Prince Philip of Greece made Duke of Edinburgh when he married George's daughter Princess Elizabeth (the current queen).
"If it were the other way around wouldn't the King's wife become a Queen and not remain a Princess?"
Yes, but that's just one of the weird sexist vagaries of the British system of titles.
If and when the current Prince of Wales ascends the throne, Camilla will not be known as HM the Queen, though that will be legally her title. The palace announced at their marriage that she will instead be known as HRH the Princess Consort (ther idea being not to upset the many people who still dislike her and feel that title should rightly have been held only by Diana, Princess of Wales, if Charles becomes king). But given the palace's propensity for waiting for people to get used to things when it comes to Camilla--and given the fact that the British people are pretty well used to her by now--my guess is the palace will change their mind if the current Queen dies and Camilla will indeed be styled HM Queen Camilla, which will in any case be her legal title.
